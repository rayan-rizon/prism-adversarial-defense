\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate \prism on CIFAR-10 against white-box, black-box,
optimization-based, and adaptive attacks, followed by per-channel ablation,
matched-FPR baseline comparison, campaign-detection latency, and L3
recovery. To assess cross-architecture and cross-dataset generalization,
we additionally report the same detection and baseline protocol on
CIFAR-10/WRN-28-10 and CIFAR-100/ResNet-18
(\S\ref{sec:multi_backbone}, Table~\ref{tab:main_multi}); a full per-table
CIFAR-100 set (adaptive PGD, ablation, recovery, campaign, latency) is
deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:cifar100}. We also include a
CIFAR-10/ViT-B/16 transformer-transfer run for FGSM, PGD-50, and Square
only; ViT latency, CW-L2, AutoAttack, and adaptive PGD are not claimed.

% ── Setup ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Base classifier.}
The main CIFAR-10 results use a CIFAR-native ResNet-18 \cite{he2016deep}
trained from scratch. For cross-architecture validation we additionally
train a WRN-28-10 \cite{zagoruyko2016wide} on CIFAR-10; for cross-dataset
validation we use ResNet-18 on CIFAR-100. \prism monitors activations at
$L=3$ depth slices weighted 0.30, 0.30, 0.40 (shallow-to-deep). For the
ResNet-18 backbones (CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100) these are \texttt{layer2},
\texttt{layer3}, \texttt{layer4}; for the WRN-28-10 backbone (which has
three residual stages, not four) the analogous depth slice is
\texttt{layer1}, \texttt{layer2}, \texttt{layer3} with the same
0.30/0.30/0.40 weights, preserving the shallow-to-deep emphasis.
For these CNN settings, all inputs remain native $3\times 32\times 32$
images with dataset-specific CIFAR normalization; no ImageNet pretraining
or 224x224 upsampling is used. For the transformer-transfer run, we
fine-tune torchvision ViT-B/16 \cite{dosovitskiy2021image} from
ImageNet \cite{deng2009imagenet} at $224\times224$ on CIFAR-10 and
monitor \texttt{encoder\_layer\_3},
\texttt{encoder\_layer\_7}, and \texttt{encoder\_layer\_11} with the same
0.30/0.30/0.40 weights. The ViT backbone records test accuracy $0.9832$
and passes an independent $n{=}1000$ verification gate at $0.9860$.

\paragraph{PRISM calibration.}
We calibrate thresholds using $n_\text{cal}=2{,}000$ held-out clean images
(split conformal: $n_\text{cal}$ for threshold fitting,
$n_\text{val}=1{,}000$ for coverage verification),
with $\alpha \in \{0.10, 0.03, 0.005\}$.
Thresholds and validation FPRs are generated by the artifact pipeline and
reported from the resulting JSON files rather than manually transcribed.

\paragraph{Attacks.}
We evaluate the following attack classes, with exact per-setting scope
reported in Table~\ref{tab:main_multi} and Appendix~\ref{app:cifar100}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{FGSM} \cite{goodfellow2015explaining}: single-step gradient attack, $\varepsilon=8/255$ ($\ell_\infty$).
  \item \textbf{PGD} \cite{madry2017towards}: projected gradient descent, $\varepsilon=8/255$, step size $\alpha=\varepsilon/4$ ($\ell_\infty$). The CIFAR-10/ResNet-18 main table uses PGD-40 (40 steps, 1 restart, the ablation-pipeline config); WRN-28-10 and CIFAR-100 use the RobustBench-standard PGD-50 (50 steps, 10 restarts). Appendix~\ref{app:audit} verifies CIFAR-10 detection is invariant to PGD-50$\times$10 ($0.991$ vs.\ $0.987$).
  \item \textbf{CW-L2} \cite{carlini2017adversarial}: optimization-based attack, canonical configuration with 100 iterations and 9 binary-search steps, matching Table~\ref{tab:main}. A weakened-CW run (5 iterations, 5 binary-search steps) is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:weak_cw} for detector-fidelity analysis only.
  \item \textbf{Square Attack} \cite{andriushchenko2020square}: black-box score-based attack, 5000 queries, $\varepsilon=8/255$ ($\ell_\infty$).
  \item \textbf{AutoAttack} \cite{croce2020reliable}: standard ensemble evaluation.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive PGD}: on CIFAR-10/ResNet-18, ensemble-complete BPDA-style attack with activation matching, side-channel surrogates, $\lambda$ sweep, 100 steps, 10 restarts, and EOT verification metadata. WRN and CIFAR-100 adaptive rows are appendix stress probes with narrower coverage.
\end{itemize}
The ViT-B/16 transfer run is intentionally narrower: FGSM, PGD-50
(50 steps, 10 restarts), and Square (5000 queries), each at
$\varepsilon=8/255$ over the same 5 seeds and $n{=}1000$/seed.
All attacks are implemented via Adversarial Robustness Toolbox (ART) v1.20 
\cite{nicolae2018adversarial} or native PyTorch implementations in pixel
$[0,1]$ space; \prism receives CIFAR-normalized inputs after adversarial generation.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We reproduce four matched-FPR baselines on the same splits:
\textbf{LID} \cite{ma2018characterizing}, \textbf{Mahalanobis}
\cite{lee2018simple}, \textbf{ODIN} \cite{liang2018enhancing}, and
\textbf{Energy} \cite{liu2020energy}.

\paragraph{Cross-setting take-aways.}
(i)~\prism transfers to a deeper architecture (WRN-28-10) without
re-tuning: the WRN run lifts every CIFAR-10 attack TPR while holding
FPR at $0.073$, indicating that the discriminative signal is in the
ensemble channels, not in a ResNet-18-specific calibration.
(ii)~Cross-dataset to CIFAR-100 (10$\times$ more classes), white-box
gradient attacks remain trivially detectable (PGD-50 $0.984$,
AutoAttack $0.999$), but score-based Square ($0.672$) and the
optimisation-based CW-L2 ($0.687$) drop below the $0.85$ design target.
Per-channel ablation (Appendix~\ref{app:cifar100}, Table~\ref{tab:c100_abl})

per-dataset retuning of stability features as a natural next step.
(iii)~The ViT-B/16 transfer run clears the standard-attack gate with
FGSM $1.000$, PGD-50 $1.000$, and Square $0.9998$ TPR at FPR $0.0828$,

residual blocks. This row is not evidence for ViT latency, CW-L2,
AutoAttack, or adaptive robustness because those runs were not executed.
(iv)~Even where \prism underperforms its $0.85$ target on CIFAR-100,
it still beats the strongest baseline on every CIFAR-100 attack
(Square: $+22.7$~pp vs ODIN; FGSM: $+6.8$~pp vs ODIN).

L3 trade-off on CIFAR-100 in Figure~\ref{fig:tiers_panel} (subfigure
\ref{fig:tiers_c100}) reflects the larger 100-class label space.

% ── Adaptive attacks ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Adaptive Attack Evaluation}
\label{sec:adaptive}

Following Carlini et al.~\cite{carlini2019evaluating}, we evaluate
\prism against a white-box adaptive attacker that crafts perturbations
explicitly suppressing the detector's signals. The canonical adaptive
attack used to generate Table~\ref{tab:adaptive_pgd} is the
ensemble-complete BPDA-style PGD launched with
\texttt{run\_adaptive\_pgd.py --through-scorer --ensemble-complete},
whose loss is
\[
  \mathcal{L} = -\mathcal{L}_\text{CE}(x', y_\text{pred})
    \;+\; \lambda \sum_{\ell} \tfrac{1}{D_\ell}\|a_\ell(x') - a_\ell(x)\|_2
    \;+\; \beta_m \|\phi(x')-\phi(x)\|_1
    \;+\; \beta_s q_\theta(x'),
\]
where $a_\ell(x)$ is the per-sample clean activation at monitored layer
$\ell$, $\phi$ is a differentiable surrogate vector for the deployed
ensemble side channels (DCT high-frequency energy, softmax entropy,
logit profile, StabilityV2, and input gradient norm), and
$q_\theta$ is the fitted side-quadratic logistic surrogate used to
attack cross-channel interactions in the ensemble scorer. Thus
$\lambda{=}0$ is not non-adaptive PGD: the ensemble side-channel
surrogate remains active, while $\lambda$ controls the additional
activation-matching pressure on \tamm. We first sweep
$\lambda \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10\}$ at $100$ PGD steps with $10$ random
restarts on a small $5{\times}50$ Monte Carlo scan, then confirm the
worst setting ($\lambda{=}10$) on $5{\times}200$ samples. Runs use the
deterministic \prism forward pass (deterministic scoring and
hash-based TDA subsampling), so EOT gradient averaging is a mathematical
no-op: the per-seed metadata records the intended verification setting
(\texttt{eot\_samples}=1, \texttt{eot\_verify\_samples}=20) but the attack
is \emph{not} re-run with $20$ resampling passes, because repeated draws
would produce identical gradients. A confirmatory
\texttt{--eot-samples 20} re-run is left as a (no-op) verification step.

\paragraph{Honest framing.} The ensemble-complete surrogate is a much
stronger attacker than the earlier two-channel \texttt{--through-scorer}
loss: it jointly attacks TDA activation matching and differentiable
surrogates for the scorer's non-TDA side channels. It substantially
reduces all-input CIFAR-10 detection, with confirmed TPR
$0.479$ at $\lambda{=}10$. However, because many high-$\lambda$
perturbations no longer fool the classifier, attack success rate also
falls to $0.552$; conditioning on successful adversarials, \prism still
detects $0.866$ and leaves $0.074$ of all attempted inputs as both
successful and undetected. FPR is governed by the fixed conformal
threshold: the held-out calibration gate passes at L1/L2/L3 FPR
$0.079/0.026/0.003$, while one $n{=}200$ confirmation seed has a noisy
L3 estimate of $2/200=0.010$ and the pooled confirmation is exactly
$0.005$. We therefore frame the confirmation as a five-seed Monte Carlo
stress test, not a disjoint-$1000$ clean benchmark.

% ── Direct surrogate-free score attack ────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Direct Surrogate-Free Score Attack and Campaign Rescue}
\label{sec:direct_score}

The adaptive attack of \S\ref{sec:adaptive} attacks differentiable
\emph{surrogates} for the non-TDA side channels, leaving open the objection
that the surrogate gap---not robustness---explains residual detection. We
close this with a \emph{surrogate-free} attack that estimates the gradient of
the \emph{exact deployed} score $S(x)$ by NES finite differences (antithetic
sampling, $Q{=}12$ pairs, $\sigma{=}0.01$) and ascends the combined objective
$J(x) = \mathcal{L}_\text{CE}(f(x),y) - c\,S(x)$, with both gradients
\emph{unit-normalised} before combining so the trade-off weight $c$ acts as a
true dial rather than an on/off switch. The classifier is white-box; the
detector is reachable only through its scalar score. All runs use
$\varepsilon{=}8/255$ and $n{=}100$ evaluation images sampled (seed $42$)
from the held-out evaluation window (test indices $[8000,10000)$).

\paragraph{The campaign monitor is load-bearing.} The surrogate-free attack
needs ${\approx}960$ score queries per image to converge, so the attack is
\emph{itself a campaign}. With the deployed \sacd CUSUM monitor live
(\texttt{models/l0\_thresholds.pkl}; every NES probe flows through it; the
monitor is \emph{reset per image}, the hardest case for the defender), the
full system catches what the single-shot tier alone misses
(Table~\ref{tab:direct_score_sacd}).

\paragraph{Scope.} This attack adapts against $S(x)$, not against \sacd. A
\emph{dual-adaptive} attacker that ramps slowly to stay under the CUSUM drift
floor is untested and left as future work; the flat $0.03$ floor suggests it
is hard, because per-sample tier evasion requires exactly the many queries
that trip the campaign monitor. This is the operative form of contribution
C3 (\S\ref{sec:intro}): \sacd is load-bearing for adaptive robustness, not an
auxiliary feature.

% ── Ablation ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

We ablate seven configurations:
(1)~Full \prism;
(2--5)~four channel-mask arms that zero one ensemble feature family at
scoring time --- No-LogitProfile, No-StabilityV2, No-GradNorm, No-DCT ---
each isolating a distinct channel's contribution;
(6)~Ensemble-no-TDA, which removes persistence features while keeping
softmax-entropy, gradient-norm, DCT, and stability channels; and
(7)~TDA-only with its own base-Wasserstein conformal calibrator.
The channel-mask design replaces an earlier "No MoE" arm that was
statistically indistinguishable from Full \prism ($t{=}0$, $p{=}1$ across all
attacks) because the MoE router is a downstream gating module that does not
alter ensemble scores; the new arms instead surface each feature family's
attack-specific failure mode (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). SACD/L0 is
evaluated separately in campaign streams because it is a temporal mechanism,
not an IID detector.

\paragraph{Baseline comparison.}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines} reports four baseline detectors at the same
$\alpha{=}0.10$ design target, aggregated across the same 5 seeds. \prism
Full has both the lowest empirical FPR ($0.074$) and the highest mean TPR
($0.918$), outperforming the strongest baseline (Mahalanobis, mean TPR
$0.582$ at FPR $0.077$) by $+33.6$~pp mean TPR. PRISM is the only
detector that does not collapse on at least one attack family
(ODIN/Energy: PGD TPR $\approx 0.001$; LID: Square TPR $0.170$).
Energy's empirical FPR ($0.105$) modestly exceeds its $\alpha{=}0.10$
design target on this test split, which we report unfiltered; recomputing
at a tighter conformal $\alpha{=}0.075$ (matched to \prism's empirical
FPR) would lower Energy's mean TPR further.
\emph{Supervision disclosure.} \prism is a supervised detector (its ensemble
head is trained on an FGSM/PGD/Square mix, \S\ref{sec:tamm}), whereas the four
detectors here are run as unsupervised variants (clean-only threshold
calibration); part of \prism's margin reflects this supervision. For a
like-for-like comparison against \emph{supervised} detectors we benchmark
SpectralDefense in Appendix~\ref{app:recent_baselines}, where \prism still wins
--- decisively on CW/AutoAttack, which it never trains on.
We additionally benchmark two recent (2021) detectors---SID
\cite{tian2021detecting} and the supervised SpectralDefense
\cite{harder2021spectraldefense}---in Appendix~\ref{app:recent_baselines}.
Both underperform \prism: SID across all attack families, and
SpectralDefense everywhere except the gradient-$L_\infty$ attacks it is
trained on, collapsing on black-box (Square) and $L_2$ (CW) attacks even
in-distribution (Table~\ref{tab:recent_baselines}) and failing to transfer
across attack families (Table~\ref{tab:spectral_cross}).

% ── Campaign detection ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Campaign Detection (SACD): Latency, Not ASR Gap}
\label{sec:campaign}

We evaluate L0 campaign detection on clean-only, sustained ($\rho \in
\{0.5, 0.8, 1.0\}$), low-rate ($\rho{=}0.05$), and burst stream scenarios
generated by \texttt{run\_campaign\_eval.py}.

\paragraph{Headline metric: time-to-detect.}
On sustained $\rho{=}1.0$ streams, the L0 monitor flags the campaign within
a mean of $\mathbf{2.6 \pm 0.5}$ queries after the first adversarial step
(across 5 seeds; Table~\ref{tab:campaign}). On $\rho{=}0.5$ streams,
detection takes $3.4 \pm 0.9$ queries; on 10-step burst streams,
$12.0 \pm 0.0$ queries; on low-rate $\rho{=}0.05$ streams,
$17.4 \pm 8.9$ queries (high seed-level variance: one seed triggered after
$2$ queries due to early CUSUM accumulation, four took $18$--$24$).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\input{tables/campaign.tex}
\caption{%
  Campaign detection across stream scenarios (5 seeds, post-fix). The
  headline metric is mean time-to-detect; clean-only L0 activation fraction
  is 0.000 on all 5 seeds after the F1 CUSUM drift-floor fix.
}
\label{tab:campaign}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Why time-to-detect, not ASR gap.}
A naive headline metric ---
adversarial success rate (ASR) reduction with L0 on vs.\ off at
$\rho{=}1.0$ --- is structurally bounded by the upstream L3 conformal
threshold, which already rejects approximately 99\% of $\rho{=}1.0$
adversarials within 22 queries, leaving no room for L0 to contribute an
additional ASR-reduction gap. We therefore report ASR gap in the appendix
for completeness, but treat detection latency (time-to-first-trigger after
campaign onset) as the operationally meaningful metric: a defender that
learns of an attack within 3 queries can deploy mitigations
(rate-limiting, account quarantine, model rollback) before the attacker
has issued more than a handful of queries against the deployed system.

\paragraph{Clean-only false alarm rate.}
After the F1 CUSUM drift-floor fix, L0 activation fraction on the
500-step calibration clean streams is $0.000$ on all 5 seeds (pre-fix:
up to $0.594$ on 2 of 5 seeds, a defect that would not have survived
peer review pre-fix). For 50-query clean windows (warmup$=20$, 30
at-risk steps), empirical false alarm probability is $< 5\%$.
A stress test on longer clean streams (bootstrap-resampled from
$n{=}1{,}000$ real post-fusion ensemble scores generated end-to-end on
the VAL split) confirms that the deployed $C^*=5.0$ holds the
$0.000$ active-fraction through $1{,}000$ steps and starts admitting
its first spurious trigger between step $\approx 1{,}800$ and step
$\approx 4{,}500$ on real long streams
(Appendix~\ref{app:l0_stress}, Table~\ref{tab:l0_stress}). The
alternative feasible cells $C^* \in \{8.0, 10.0, 12.0\}$ from the
calibration grid hold $0.000$ across all stream lengths up to
$10{,}000$ steps. Longer-window deployments should either pick one of
these conservative cells or periodically reset L0 / auto-deactivate on
return to the clean score baseline.

\paragraph{Two distinct false-alarm rates.}
The \emph{FPR (clean steps)} column in Table~\ref{tab:campaign}
($0.084$--$0.143$) is the per-query L1/L2/L3 conformal alert rate of the
underlying IID detector --- the same FPR reported in
Table~\ref{tab:main} --- not the L0 campaign-trigger rate. A deployment
that quarantines accounts should gate on L0 firing (clean-only rate
$0.000$ after the F1 fix), not on every L1 step; L1 alerts are
appropriate for softer signals such as elevated logging or rate-limiting,
where a $\sim$10\% per-query rate is acceptable because the action is
reversible and per-query.

% ── Recovery (TAMSH) ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{L3 Recovery (TAMSH)}
\label{sec:recovery}

We measure recovery accuracy of five routing strategies applied to the
subset of PGD inputs that trigger the L3 (reject) tier: the most aggressive
adversarials, where simply rejecting the input is the conservative default.
We pre-registered a $+15$~pp recovery gap (best router vs.\
passthrough) as the contribution target for this component. The lightweight
topology gate (combined $H_0{+}H_1$ Wasserstein) falls $4.4$~pp short of
that target at $+10.6$~pp (Table~\ref{tab:recovery}); a \emph{learned}
router trained on the L3 pool (logistic regression on final-layer
activations) clears the target, recovering $0.287$ --- $9.1\times$ the
uniform $1/K$ control and $2.1\times$ the topology gate, matching the
prior-augmented upper bound without hardcoding the attack
(Appendix~\ref{app:tamsh_diag}).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\input{tables/recovery.tex}
\caption{%
  L3-recovery on PGD adversarials (5 seeds, $n_{L3} \approx 792$ per
  seed; mean trigger rate $0.792$ within sanity band $[0.10, 0.82]$). The
  \texttt{tamsh} row is the lightweight topology gate (combined
  $H_0{+}H_1$), which delivers $+10.6$~pp over passthrough, falling
  $4.4$~pp short of the pre-registered $+15$~pp target; a learned router
  trained on the L3 pool clears the target (Appendix~\ref{app:tamsh_diag}).
  The \texttt{tamsh\_force} row is a prior-augmented upper-bound analysis

  ceiling is per-input best-of-$K$ on the same L3 subset.
}
\label{tab:recovery}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Router contribution isolation.}
The \texttt{tamsh\_uniform} arm replaces topology-distance gating with
uniform $1/K$ mixing across all 4 experts. Its recovery accuracy equals
that of passthrough ($0.033$ each, paired gap $0.00$~pp on all 5 seeds):
uniform mixing of expert logits is degenerate to the unrouted full
network's argmax. The $+10.6$~pp \texttt{tamsh} gap (combined $H_0{+}H_1$
Wasserstein gating) over uniform is therefore attributable entirely to the
routing signal, not to standalone expert-network capacity --- a positive
but partial result against the pre-registered $+15$~pp target. The further
$+15.4$~pp lift achieved by \texttt{tamsh\_force} (always-route to the
PGD-trained expert) reflects correct routing under a strong prior: L3-
rejected inputs are by definition high-confidence adversarials, and PGD
is the dominant adversarial signal in our training mix. We treat
\texttt{tamsh\_force} as an upper-bound analysis rather than a deployable
policy, since it bakes in an oracle assumption about attack identity that
a real router would have to learn online. The gap between the topology
gate ($0.139$ recovery accuracy) and the prior-augmented variant
($0.293$) bounds the headroom for a stronger gating signal below the
per-input best-of-$K$ oracle ceiling ($0.324 \pm 0.007$, recomputed on the
same-protocol 5-seed PGD L3 pool, $n_{L3}{\approx}796$/seed,
App.~\ref{app:tamsh_diag}); a \emph{learned} router trained on the L3 pool
closes most of it, reaching $0.287$ (matching the prior-augmented variant
without its hardcoded prior; Appendix~\ref{app:tamsh_diag}).

% ── Certificate under benign covariate shift ──────────────────────────────
\subsection{Certificate Validity Under Benign Covariate Shift (CIFAR-10-C)}
\label{sec:cert_shift}

The \cadg guarantee (Proposition~\ref{prop:coverage}) is exact only under
exchangeability: the clean test distribution must match the calibration
distribution. We audit what happens when this assumption is \emph{benignly}
violated, using CIFAR-10-C \cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}: 8 corruption
families (gaussian noise, shot noise, defocus blur, motion blur, fog, frost,
contrast, JPEG compression) at severities $\{1,3,5\}$, $n{=}1000$ images per
cell (24 cells, forward passes only, seed 42). Every non-PASS verdict on a
corrupted-but-benign input counts as a false positive against the certified
$\alpha$.

\paragraph{Reading.} The certificate should be advertised as
\emph{in-distribution} FPR control. Under benign corruption it fails in the
flagging direction --- corrupted inputs look anomalous to the same channels
that flag adversarials --- so the system remains safe but loses availability;
the measured degradation in Table~\ref{tab:cert_shift} replaces the
qualitative caveat of earlier drafts and bounds the operational cost of
deploying \prism under shift without recalibration.
