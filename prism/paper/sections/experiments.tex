\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate \prism on CIFAR-10 against white-box, black-box,
optimization-based, and adaptive attacks, followed by per-channel ablation,
matched-FPR baseline comparison, campaign-detection latency, and L3
recovery. The same protocol applied to CIFAR-100 produces a parallel set
of tables; per-attack CIFAR-100 numbers are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:cifar100} once the locked Vast.ai pipeline
(\texttt{run\_vastai\_cifar100.sh}) completes.

% ── Setup ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Base classifier.}
We use a CIFAR-native ResNet-18 \cite{he2016deep} trained from scratch on
the active dataset.
\prism monitors activations at $L=3$ residual block outputs
(\texttt{layer2}, \texttt{layer3}, \texttt{layer4}) weighted 0.30, 0.30, 0.40.
All inputs remain native $3\times 32\times 32$ images with dataset-specific
CIFAR normalization; no ImageNet pretraining or 224x224 upsampling is used.

\paragraph{PRISM calibration.}
We calibrate thresholds using $n_\text{cal}=2{,}000$ held-out clean images
(split conformal: $n_\text{cal}$ for threshold fitting,
$n_\text{val}=1{,}000$ for coverage verification),
with $\alpha \in \{0.10, 0.03, 0.005\}$.
Thresholds and validation FPRs are generated by the artifact pipeline and
reported from the resulting JSON files rather than manually transcribed.

\paragraph{Attacks.}
We evaluate four attack classes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{FGSM} \cite{goodfellow2015explaining}: single-step gradient attack, $\varepsilon=8/255$ ($\ell_\infty$).
  \item \textbf{PGD-40} \cite{madry2017towards}: 40-step projected gradient descent, $\varepsilon=8/255$, step size $\alpha=\varepsilon/4$ ($\ell_\infty$).
  \item \textbf{CW-L2} \cite{carlini2017adversarial}: optimization-based attack, 40 iterations and 5 binary-search steps in the Vast.ai gate run.
  \item \textbf{Square Attack} \cite{andriushchenko2020square}: black-box score-based attack, 5000 queries, $\varepsilon=8/255$ ($\ell_\infty$).
  \item \textbf{AutoAttack} \cite{croce2020reliable}: standard ensemble evaluation.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive PGD}: BPDA-style activation-matching attack with $\lambda$ sweep, 100 steps, 10 restarts, and EOT verification metadata.
\end{itemize}
All attacks are implemented via Adversarial Robustness Toolbox (ART) v1.20 
\cite{nicolae2018adversarial} or native PyTorch implementations in pixel
$[0,1]$ space; \prism receives CIFAR-normalized inputs after adversarial generation.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We reproduce four matched-FPR baselines on the same splits:
\textbf{LID} \cite{ma2018characterizing}, \textbf{Mahalanobis}
\cite{lee2018simple}, \textbf{ODIN} \cite{liang2018enhancing}, and
\textbf{Energy} \cite{liu2020energy}.

% ── Adaptive attacks ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Adaptive Attack Evaluation}
\label{sec:adaptive}

Following Carlini et al.~\cite{carlini2019evaluating}, we evaluate \prism
against a white-box adaptive attacker who has access to the Wasserstein
scoring channel and crafts perturbations that explicitly suppress its
signal. The attack is a BPDA-style PGD that augments the classification
loss with a weighted activation-matching term
$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_\text{CE}(x', y) - \lambda \cdot
\|\phi_\ell(x') - \mu_\ell\|_2$, where $\mu_\ell$ is the clean-profile
medoid activation at the monitored layer $\ell$ and $\lambda$ controls
the trade-off between misclassification and \prism-evasion. We sweep
$\lambda \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10\}$ at 100 PGD steps with 10 random
restarts and verify success via EOT-style stochastic re-evaluation.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\input{tables/adaptive_pgd.tex}
\caption{%
  Adaptive PGD with activation-matching term ($\lambda$ sweep, 100 steps,
  10 restarts, 5 seeds). TPR degrades monotonically as $\lambda$ grows,
  bottoming at $0.429$ at $\lambda{=}10$ while FPR holds at the calibrated
  $0.075$.
}
\label{tab:adaptive_pgd}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Honest framing.} As $\lambda$ grows, an attacker who is willing
to spend perturbation budget on activation-matching can substantially
reduce \prism's detection rate (TPR drops from $0.832$ at $\lambda{=}0$
to $0.429$ at $\lambda{=}10$); FPR is unaffected because the conformal
threshold is fixed on clean calibration scores and does not depend on the
adversary's loss. The reported attack targets the Wasserstein channel
specifically; a fully end-to-end adaptive attack that also penalises the
DCT-energy, gradient-norm, and softmax-entropy ensemble channels is
implemented in \texttt{run\_adaptive\_pgd.py} via the
\texttt{--through-scorer} flag and is left for future evaluation. We do
not claim adaptive-attack robustness; instead, we report degradation
curves so deployments can choose operating points and so future work can
benchmark against a documented baseline.

% ── Ablation ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

We ablate seven configurations:
(1)~Full \prism;
(2--5)~four channel-mask arms that zero one ensemble feature family at
scoring time --- No-LogitProfile, No-StabilityV2, No-GradNorm, No-DCT ---
each isolating a distinct channel's contribution;
(6)~Ensemble-no-TDA, which removes persistence features while keeping
softmax-entropy, gradient-norm, DCT, and stability channels; and
(7)~TDA-only with its own base-Wasserstein conformal calibrator.
The channel-mask design replaces an earlier "No MoE" arm that was
statistically indistinguishable from Full \prism ($t{=}0$, $p{=}1$ across all
attacks) because the MoE router is a downstream gating module that does not
alter ensemble scores; the new arms instead surface each feature family's
attack-specific failure mode (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). SACD/L0 is
evaluated separately in campaign streams because it is a temporal mechanism,
not an IID detector.

\paragraph{Baseline comparison.}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines} reports four baseline detectors at the same
$\alpha{=}0.10$ design target, aggregated across the same 5 seeds. \prism
Full has both the lowest empirical FPR ($0.074$) and the highest mean TPR
($0.918$), outperforming the strongest baseline (Mahalanobis, mean TPR
$0.582$ at FPR $0.077$) by $+33.6$~pp mean TPR. PRISM is the only
detector that does not collapse on at least one attack family
(ODIN/Energy: PGD TPR $\approx 0.001$; LID: Square TPR $0.170$).
Energy's empirical FPR ($0.105$) modestly exceeds its $\alpha{=}0.10$
design target on this test split, which we report unfiltered; recomputing
at a tighter conformal $\alpha{=}0.075$ (matched to \prism's empirical
FPR) would lower Energy's mean TPR further.

% ── Campaign detection ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Campaign Detection (SACD): Latency, Not ASR Gap}
\label{sec:campaign}

We evaluate L0 campaign detection on clean-only, sustained ($\rho \in
\{0.5, 0.8, 1.0\}$), low-rate ($\rho{=}0.05$), and burst stream scenarios
generated by \texttt{run\_campaign\_eval.py}.

\paragraph{Headline metric: time-to-detect.}
On sustained $\rho{=}1.0$ streams, the L0 monitor flags the campaign within
a mean of $\mathbf{2.6 \pm 0.5}$ queries after the first adversarial step
(across 5 seeds; Table~\ref{tab:campaign}). On $\rho{=}0.5$ streams,
detection takes $3.4 \pm 0.9$ queries; on 10-step burst streams,
$12.0 \pm 0.0$ queries; on low-rate $\rho{=}0.05$ streams,
$17.4 \pm 8.9$ queries (high seed-level variance: one seed triggered after
$2$ queries due to early CUSUM accumulation, four took $18$--$24$).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\input{tables/campaign.tex}
\caption{%
  Campaign detection across stream scenarios (5 seeds, post-fix). The
  headline metric is mean time-to-detect; clean-only L0 activation fraction
  is 0.000 on all 5 seeds after the F1 CUSUM drift-floor fix.
}
\label{tab:campaign}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Why time-to-detect, not ASR gap.}
A naive headline metric ---
adversarial success rate (ASR) reduction with L0 on vs.\ off at
$\rho{=}1.0$ --- is structurally bounded by the upstream L3 conformal
threshold, which already rejects approximately 99\% of $\rho{=}1.0$
adversarials within 22 queries, leaving no room for L0 to contribute an
additional ASR-reduction gap. We therefore report ASR gap in the appendix
for completeness, but treat detection latency (time-to-first-trigger after
campaign onset) as the operationally meaningful metric: a defender that
learns of an attack within 3 queries can deploy mitigations
(rate-limiting, account quarantine, model rollback) before the attacker
has issued more than a handful of queries against the deployed system.

\paragraph{Clean-only false alarm rate.}
After the F1 CUSUM drift-floor fix, L0 activation fraction on clean-only
streams is $0.000$ on all 5 seeds (pre-fix: up to $0.594$ on 2 of 5 seeds,
a defect that would not have survived peer review pre-fix). For 50-query
clean windows (warmup$=35$, 15 at-risk steps), empirical false alarm
probability is $< 5\%$; longer-window deployments should periodically reset
L0 or auto-deactivate on return to the clean score baseline.

\paragraph{Two distinct false-alarm rates.}
The \emph{FPR (clean steps)} column in Table~\ref{tab:campaign}
($0.084$--$0.143$) is the per-query L1/L2/L3 conformal alert rate of the
underlying IID detector --- the same FPR reported in
Table~\ref{tab:main} --- not the L0 campaign-trigger rate. A deployment
that quarantines accounts should gate on L0 firing (clean-only rate
$0.000$ after the F1 fix), not on every L1 step; L1 alerts are
appropriate for softer signals such as elevated logging or rate-limiting,
where a $\sim$10\% per-query rate is acceptable because the action is
reversible and per-query.

% ── Recovery (TAMSH) ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{L3 Recovery (TAMSH)}
\label{sec:recovery}

We measure recovery accuracy of five routing strategies applied to the
subset of PGD inputs that trigger the L3 (reject) tier: the most aggressive
adversarials, where simply rejecting the input is the conservative default.
We pre-registered a $+15$~pp recovery gap (best learned router vs.\
passthrough) as the contribution target for this component. As reported
below, the \emph{learned} router (combined $H_0{+}H_1$ Wasserstein gating)
falls $4.4$~pp short of that target at $+10.6$~pp; we therefore report this
component as a positive-signal contribution rather than a target-clearing
result, and document the residual gap to the prior-augmented variant and
the oracle ceiling as the path for future learned routing.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\input{tables/recovery.tex}
\caption{%
  L3-recovery on PGD adversarials (5 seeds, $n_{L3} \approx 792$ per
  seed; mean trigger rate $0.792$ within sanity band $[0.10, 0.82]$). The
  learned-router row (\texttt{tamsh}, combined $H_0{+}H_1$) is the
  primary contribution and delivers $+10.6$~pp over passthrough, falling
  $4.4$~pp short of the pre-registered $+15$~pp target. The
  \texttt{tamsh\_force} row is a prior-augmented upper-bound analysis
  (always-route to PGD specialist, not a learned policy); the oracle
  ceiling is per-input best-of-$K$ on the same L3 subset.
}
\label{tab:recovery}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Router contribution isolation.}
The \texttt{tamsh\_uniform} arm replaces topology-distance gating with
uniform $1/K$ mixing across all 4 experts. Its recovery accuracy equals
that of passthrough ($0.033$ each, paired gap $0.00$~pp on all 5 seeds):
uniform mixing of expert logits is degenerate to the unrouted full
network's argmax. The $+10.6$~pp \texttt{tamsh} gap (combined $H_0{+}H_1$
Wasserstein gating) over uniform is therefore attributable entirely to the
routing signal, not to standalone expert-network capacity --- a positive
but partial result against the pre-registered $+15$~pp target. The further
$+15.4$~pp lift achieved by \texttt{tamsh\_force} (always-route to the
PGD-trained expert) reflects correct routing under a strong prior: L3-
rejected inputs are by definition high-confidence adversarials, and PGD
is the dominant adversarial signal in our training mix. We treat
\texttt{tamsh\_force} as an upper-bound analysis rather than a deployable
policy, since it bakes in an oracle assumption about attack identity that
a real router would have to learn online. The gap between the learned
router ($0.139$ recovery accuracy) and the prior-augmented variant
($0.293$) bounds the headroom achievable by a stronger gating signal
below the oracle ceiling ($0.399$).

\subsection{Calibration Sensitivity}
\label{sec:calibration}

Figure~\ref{fig:calib} sweeps $n_\text{cal} \in \{500, 1000, 2000, 3000\}$
and confirms threshold stability: the L3 threshold varies by $\pm 0.03$
(range $0.06$) across calibration set sizes (25 bootstrap subsamples per
size), and the achieved FPR on the held-out validation split tracks the
target $\alpha$ within the Wilson-95\% interval, satisfying
Proposition~\ref{prop:coverage}.
